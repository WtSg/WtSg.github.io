How it works, in four steps

1

The lens brings the light to a point

The lens is a stack of glass, here six elements in four groups. Each curved surface bends the light a little, so the light that spreads out from one point of your subject is gathered and brought back together at one point on the film. Between the two halves of the glass, the aperture blades set how wide that bundle of light can be.

f/2

Each step closes the blades a little: it lets in half as much light, and the light from things nearer or further than the focus spreads less, so more of the scene is sharp.

2

Focusing moves the glass

The nearer the subject, the further behind the lens its light comes back together. So turning the focus ring screws the whole optical unit forward on its helicoid, away from the film, until the light meets exactly on the film. Here the subject is 1 m away: with the lens at ∞ its light would meet just behind the film, and on the film it makes a blurred disc.

∞

3

The rangefinder shows when it is right

You do not look through the lens. You look through the big window beside it, and in the middle of the view a small patch shows the scene a second time, seen from the small window a few centimetres along. The focusing cam at the back of the lens moves with the glass; a roller rests on it and, through two levers, turns the mirror behind the small window. When the lens is focused on your subject, the two lines of sight meet on it, and in the patch the two images become one.

∞

Not to scale: angles drawn nine times their size.

4

The shutter lets the light onto the film

Press the release, and two curtains just in front of the film run across it, one after the other. The first opens the frame; the second follows and closes it. The light reaches the film through the slit between them: at fast speeds a narrow slit, at slow ones the whole frame, open at once. Then a stroke of the advance lever winds the film on, from its cassette across the gate onto the take-up spool, and cocks the shutter for the next picture.
