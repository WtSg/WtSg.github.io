# Surface brief: the camera, taken apart

Scope: `/photography/camera/` (`_layouts/camera.html`), a page of the photography section: how a Leica M-style rangefinder works, taken apart. The owner: "add a particular page to teardown the Leica camera for every part in my web, so it's not in this challenge"; then "illustrate everything better using your skill and put Leica lens like the element so show how light travel; just make it super intuitive, right now it's bad; make it good for each one [everyone] understand how Leica working". Visitor mode: Experience; the drawings lead. Linked from the wall's header ("The camera, taken apart") and from the focus challenge's guide (its step "Inside the camera").

Audience and job: friends, colleagues and visitors to the photography pages, mostly on a phone (the owner reviews on an iPhone). They should be able to take the camera apart, pick out any part and read what it does, then see how turning the lens tells the rangefinder where it is focused.

Proof and content: technical illustrations made with the teardown skill (`_teardown/rangefinder`, evidence in its `evidence.md`): illustrations of the classic rangefinder layout, not any one camera's construction, with no maker's name or mark. The lens is a classic double-Gauss form (six elements in four groups, the textbook design scaled to 50 mm), named for no lens; the light through it is traced with Snell's law. The part descriptions (`_data/camera_parts.yml`) say what each part is for, with no specifications, materials or one camera's construction. No photographs on this page. "Leica M-style" in the text only; the URL and file names say "camera".

Constraints: plain Jekyll as GitHub Pages builds it; no WebGL; the page zooms; drawings are pre-rendered frames (JSON) swapped by sliders, their lines the same width on screen at any size; reduced motion gets still states and no auto-play; without JavaScript every part is still listed with its text.

## Direction contract

THESIS: A real teardown on the photography pages' dark ground that anyone can follow: the camera comes apart in your hands, then four steps follow the light from the subject through the lens to the film, each with one drawing and one control, and every part can be picked out and read about.

OWN-WORLD: The photography pages' near-black ground, Schibsted Grotesk and Geist Mono. Fine light ink for the drawings; amber for what moves when the lens is focused; pale blue for glass; the LED red for light and the subject; the see-through shells dimmed; a pale gold for the part picked out.

LAYOUT (phone first): the name and Photography at the top; the title, a line on what the page is, the note that it is an illustration seen from behind, and three links down the page. "Take it apart": a slider over the drawing (Together, Coming apart, Apart, seven steps); a drag across the drawing does the same; the parts are drawn solid (each filled a shade darker than its ink, the shells and the glass left open), the lens's glass in its four groups and the aperture blades coming out of the front of the lens in their order; the light's path, the lens's axis dashed in red, runs through every group and the mount into the body; a small colour key under the drawing (the light's path, glass, what moves when you focus); a tap on a part, or its name in the list, picks it out (the rest dims, it and its name light up) and shows a card under the drawing (beside it on a wide screen) with the part drawn on its own, its group, its name, what it does, Previous and Next through every part, and All parts; Escape lets go; the address names the part (#part-cam); it comes apart by itself once. "How it works, in four steps", each a numbered heading, two or three sentences, one drawing and at most one control: 1. "The lens brings the light to a point": a section through the lens to the film (glass tinted pale blue, cut metal hatched, the moving optical unit one way and the fixed barrel the other, the film in its own colour), the light from one point of a subject 1 m away traced through it in the LED red; an Aperture slider (f/2 to f/16) closes the blades, which stop the outer rays (shown faint where they stop); under it, the film magnified, the light's spot on it. 2. "Focusing moves the glass": the same section; a "Lens focused at" slider (the lens's scale, ∞ to 0.7 m) slides the optical unit forward; the light meets behind the film at ∞ (shown faint, dashed, past it), on it at 1 m, in front of it nearer; the magnified spot is a blurred disc (its size given) or a sharp point; the first time, it focuses itself from ∞ to the subject. 3. "The rangefinder shows when it is right": the small finder (frame corners, the patch, a post and its second image) beside a "Lens focused at" slider, and the drawing of the rangefinder at work (shell see-through, lens, cam, roller, levers, mirror, the two lines of sight meeting on the subject at 1 m); it focuses itself the first time. 4. "The shutter lets the light onto the film": the shutter caught mid-travel seen from the lens (the two curtains in amber, the slit of light in pale gold, the light from the lens in red, the film from its cassette across the gate onto the spool), a small colour key under it, and a note that it is seen from the lens's side. "Every part" is a catalogue of the 30 parts in six groups (the top, the finder, the rangefinder, the body, the lens, the lens's glass): a grid of tiles, three across on a phone, each the part drawn small on its own with its name under it (the owner, of the earlier list of 30 text cards: "these are too messy"); a tap picks the part out in the camera taken apart and brings the card with what it does to stand right under that tile's row, with "See it in the camera ↑" back to the drawing; Previous and Next move the card along the catalogue; without JavaScript the tiles list the names and texts. On a phone the 3D drawings carry no names (the list and the keys do); on a wide screen one readable column, the drawings capped in width. Footer: the year and name, a credit for the teardown skill, Email me, Photography, Homepage.
