# Surface brief: photography, endless canvas

Scope: `/photography/canvas/` (`_layouts/gallery-canvas.html`), one of the gallery versions the owner is comparing (kept unlisted with noindex until chosen). The owner's personal photographs ("just my life"). Visitor mode: Experience; the photographs lead. It shares the photo data, the close-up viewer and the studio with the other versions, and has its own visual world, separate from the scientific homepage.

Audience and job: friends, colleagues and visitors from the research site, browsing at leisure on a laptop or phone. They should feel they are looking around a wall of the owner's photographs through a camera, settling on one at a time, and be able to open any of them large.

Proof and content: photos published by the Gallery Studio into `_data/gallery.yml` (id, src, thumb, w, h, title, place, year, optional exif). Most photos have no title, place, year or camera settings; never show file names; never invent settings. Very little text.

Constraints: plain Jekyll as GitHub Pages builds it; photographs stay plain `<img>` elements in their true colours and their own aspect ratios; only the published watermarked copies are shown; no feed-like layout; no WebGL; reduced motion gets instant, still states; without JavaScript the same photographs appear in justified rows.

## Direction contract

THESIS: The gallery is an endless wall of photographs seen through a rangefinder camera's viewfinder. You look around it, and the camera frames, focuses and takes one photograph at a time. It refuses the scrolling grid and the full-screen slideshow.

OWN-WORLD: A near-black wall of photographs that repeats in every direction. A rangefinder viewfinder sits over it: warm bright-line frame corners and a faint full outline, a small yellowish focus patch showing a ghost of the picture, and a red seven-segment LED readout under the frame (DSEG7 digits over their unlit segments, Geist Mono labels) with a three-lamp focus indicator (two arrows and a dot) and a frame counter. Outside the frame lines the wall darkens at rest, as through an eyepiece. Name and footer in Schibsted Grotesk and Geist Mono.

STORY: The camera wakes (every segment and lamp lit for a moment), pulled back over the whole wall, then finds the newest photograph, closes in until it fills the frame and focuses; the frame lines, the darkening outside them and the focus patch come up only as that photograph glides into place (the owner: "when it first launch, the square edge is not correct"), and the patch never shows as an empty box. The wall's order is the gallery's: the newest year first, the latest added first within a year ("sort by year"). The visitor drags or uses the arrow keys to look around (Ctrl or Cmd + wheel moves to the next or the previous photograph, at the owner's ask); the faster the wall moves, the further the lens pulls back. When it slows, the nearest photograph is framed and focused. Zoomed out, clicking a photograph opens it straight away (the owner, at first: "when it's small when I click it will still enter the detail not zoom in", then: "do not single click to enlarge, just single click goes to the detail"); at the framed size or closer, clicking a photograph opens it large at once, growing out of its place with no wipe across the screen (the owner: "I do t like a refresh from left to right"), and behind the close-up the wall frames the photograph clicked. Focus is shown and handed back only to keyboard users, so no label or ring appears after a tap or click. The large view is the frame, developed (the owner: "the gallery use the matching style and make it extremely cool"): the photograph alone on black inside the same bright-line frame (faint outline, bright corners), which closes in as the photograph pulls into focus; the same red LED readout under it (F, shutter, ISO, mm, frame number; every segment lit for a moment, then the digits roll and settle); a caption line (title, place, year) and the camera and lens in mono; and along the bottom the roll, every photograph as a frame on a strip of film with sprocket holes and its number printed on the edge. The strip is seen as in an open camera back (the owner: "it's no way to be the top of the film cage", then "upright side view"): it comes out of the felt light-trap slot of an upright metal cassette on the right (no brand; the knurled knob on top turns, with a red mark going round), passes under a fixed red film gate in the middle, and winds onto an upright take-up spool between two flanges on the left (the roll on it thickens as frames pass, its sprocket holes travelling round as it turns). It settles with one frame in the gate, which becomes the photograph shown: any deliberate swipe moves one frame (the owner: "the red box dynamically move to the central … when we left right sweep the film roll, it will auto move to last next photo … show a film on the left or right so when we drag the film it will rotate something just like a real film"). Any frame is clickable (a tap on a frame goes straight to it); a fast fling runs on through as many frames as its speed carries it, a short swipe still moves one (the owner: "when I move really fast scroll it will not responsive"); a mouse can drag the film or turn it a frame per wheel notch. Swiping the photograph down closes the close-up, with a finger or the mouse ("When I sweep down I can exit this detail page", "can not use mouse to drag down to close?"). A pinch (two fingers, a trackpad, Ctrl and the wheel) zooms into the photograph itself, never the page ("zoom in failure"); zoomed in, it moves about under the finger or the mouse, always covering its frame, and a tap or pinching back returns it whole. The focus patch eases with the frame lines when the wall zooms, never jumping ("this inner box is not smooth it is like stuck"). Held with the mouse button or a finger, the wall stays pulled back as far as the drag took it, closing in only once let go ("only enlarge when we release the left button"). Glass keys either side for previous and next, and Close; arrow keys and swipes step. Opening and closing move as an app does on a phone (the owner: "simulate how iOS open an app"): the close-up grows out of the photograph's place on the wall as a rounded window on a spring, and closing shrinks it back. Behind the close-up the wall follows it out of sight, framing each photograph stepped to, so closing (a tap, the Close key, or a swipe down, which flies back from where the finger left it) shrinks into the photograph now shown, never back to the first one and never a fade (the owner: "i want it will auto navigate to the 6 image in the canvas and do not blink"). Paging is a phone photo album's (the owner: "simulate Apple albums left right sweep's effect"): sideways the photographs lie side by side a gap apart, a drag moves them with the finger and the next comes in from the side, let go they glide on (a fifth of the way, or a flick) or back; arrows, keys and the roll slide the same way, and nothing fades. The frame lines stay up throughout, fixed in the middle like a viewfinder's, and ease from the shape of the photograph leaving to that of the one arriving, with the finger while dragging and with the glide after (the owner: "when I drag the image the box edge just disappears and reappears, I want a smooth transfer"). On a wide but short screen (a phone turned on its side) the close-up stands side by side: the photograph the whole height on the left, the readout, caption, camera line, roll and keys in a column beside it (the owner, of a tiny photograph above everything else: "When I vertical"). On a phone the camera and the lens sit in one fixed place just above the roll, a line each (a long name ends in an ellipsis), their room kept for a photograph that has neither, so the camera line, the roll and the keys never move from one photograph to the next (the owner: "for the bottom film can we put in the fixed location"). Clicking or tapping the close-up never closes it (the owner: "do not use click to quit, only use drag down to quit"): a click beside the photograph, on the glimpse of a neighbour or the black on that side, shows that neighbour; a click on the photograph itself does nothing ("since we added left and right image, when we click this image we do not go to next or last"); closing is a swipe or drag down, the Close key or Escape. On a trackpad two fingers work as in Photos on a Mac: sideways the photographs follow them and glide on or back, down pulls the photograph away to the wall; a mouse wheel's notch steps. On the wall two fingers on a trackpad move it, while the mouse wheel and a pinch zoom ("can we use trackpad to move the wall while using mouse wheel to zoom"). A click on a photograph beside the framed one glides it into the frame and opens it as it arrives ("when we click an image, move to center and then go to detail"); the framed one opens at once. A pinch anywhere on the page zooms the wall or the photograph, never the page itself. At rest the framed photograph is about a quarter of the window (a third for a portrait; on a phone about half the width, a third of the height for a portrait: "For iPhone by default this size"), so the photographs round it show and the page reads as a wall, the close-up being there for the detail (the owner: "show the sizing roughly like this, so we can see the side images as well, and don't make the largest this large"). The wall is seen through a lens: its corners fall off a little darker and, at rest, a touch soft. In the close-up, where there is room beside the photograph (not on a phone), a dimmed glimpse of the previous and the next one shows at the edges; they are the very pictures that slide in. Every tap on the keys is answered at once (the owner: "The left right bottom is not responsive easily"): a step never waits on the network (the photograph arriving shows its small print until the full one comes, then sharpens), a quick run of taps keeps gliding from where it is, the counter and the roll move with the tap, and the keys give under the finger. This is the main photography page (/photography/), with "Enter the darkroom" (a small safelight-red dot before it) beside the name leading to the 3D darkroom, which links "Back to the wall". Zoom by hand (added at the owner's ask: "add a feature to zoom in and zoom out (touch use 2 fingers)"): a two-finger pinch zooms and pans at once; with a trackpad, pinch; with a mouse, the wheel itself ("can we use the mouse wheel directly to control zoom"), the - and + buttons in the corner above the footer, or the - and + keys (0 resets). Zoomed out, more of the wall shows and the frame lines close round the photograph. The LED readout is part of the viewfinder: it sits just under the frame lines at every zoom and is exactly as wide as they are, its digits scaled to fit, a portrait photograph's turned frame included (the owner: "put this under the viewfinder because it belongs to the viewfinder and make it the same width as the viewfinder because it's part of the viewfinder", after it had for a while sat at the foot of the window); zoomed far out it stops shrinking where its digits would be too hard to read, and round the very smallest frame lines (a portrait photograph on a phone held sideways) it keeps a least width so they can still be read. It stands on its own, with no caption (the owner: "delete the camera detail and year, just delete the whole box"); the camera and the lens are in the close-up. Zoomed in, the wall goes no further than the framed size, the photograph filling the frame lines: a pinch or the wheel can stretch it a little past them, against a resistance that grows the further it goes, and once let go it is pulled back into them (the owner: "when image is larger than the viewfinder, just pull it back"); the + button and key stop at the framed size. A closer look is the close-up's. It still comes to rest on the nearest photograph.

FIRST VIEWPORT: Full-bleed dark wall, pulled back to show many photographs; the bright-line frame centred; the LED readout under it showing all segments; then the newest photograph glides into the frame and the lens closes in until it fills it. The name top left (linking home), the photo count top right, a one-line footer with Email me, Homepage and Studio, and a one-line hint under the readout ("Drag to look around · scroll to zoom · click to open") that fades after the first move.

FORM: Endless canvas (the owner picked it from the brainstorm: "Endless canvas is good"), given the camera mechanism for "visually shocking, better from mechanism, retro and extremely cool". Signature interactions: speed-dependent pull-back; glide-and-close-in framing; a portrait photograph turns the camera, rotating the frame lines a quarter turn; the ghost image slides into register and the focus dot lights; the frame counter rolls like a mechanical counter. Each build stamps the page's scripts and styles, so a phone never runs the last deploy's. Camera settings show on the LED only when the photograph carries them. The bright-line frame keeps its square corners and the darkening is cut square round it; the focus patch in the middle is a small rectangle, its corners rounded in proportion to it (a tenth of its short side, about 2.5px at rest; the owner: "when zoom out keep proportion", "actually 2.5 is good"), its size about an eighth of the frame's long side at any zoom and no more than about a tenth of its short side, so a panorama's is smaller ("the inner box for this kind of ratio is too large", "for others a little large too"), and upright for a portrait photograph as the frame lines turn ("when it's vertical the inner rectangle needs to be vertical as well") (the owner: "just a little in Leica extremely tiny", then "it's too large … the Leica only has an extremely small thing, nearly nothing") (the owner: "real viewfinder is actually rounded rectangle", then "I meant the inner rectangle is rounded, the outside is still original", then "The inner focus rectangle"). The photographs stay square. The camera's maker shows as its mark in the ink colour (Leica's dot in its red) before the model in the close-up (the owner: "show camera logo somewhere like leica iphone hasselblad sony canon fujifilm nikon"): marks from Simple Icons (CC0) for Leica, Apple, Sony, Fujifilm, Nikon, Panasonic, Kodak, DJI, Google and Samsung, and Hasselblad's wordmark as its own website draws it (the owner: "the Hasselblad should use its logo"); Canon and others, which Simple Icons does not carry, are set as letter-spaced wordmarks rather than copied. A mark that is only a symbol (Leica's dot, Apple's apple, Google's G, Kodak's K) is followed by the maker's name, and a mark that spells the name (SONY, FUJIFILM, HASSELBLAD, Nikon, DJI) stands alone (the owner: "After showing the camera icon still show the brand"). The name is set in lettering close to the maker's own ("use camera brands' font"): Apple in Apple's own SF where the device has it; Leica in bold capitals of Hanken Grotesk (OFL), a sober sans standing in for its licensed Corporate S. The makers' real typefaces are licensed and are not copied. Rendering: while the wall moves it is scaled as one layer; at rest the scale goes into each photograph's own size, so Safari (which keeps a scaled layer at the resolution it was drawn at) shows it sharp ("For iPhone the quality is so bad"). The darkening outside the frame is a plain veil with the frame cut out (clip-path), and the focus patch's ghost is plain translucent, with no blend mode (Safari washed photographs out while dragging). HDR photographs show standard range on the wall and glow only once framed and sharp (dynamic-range-limit). Reduced motion shows every state instantly with no inertia, speed zoom, turn or shutter (zooming by hand still works, at once).

FINISH: unreviewed and undocumented is unfinished; this build ends with the finish review, the verdict, DESIGN.md, and every shipping raster carrying its provenance
