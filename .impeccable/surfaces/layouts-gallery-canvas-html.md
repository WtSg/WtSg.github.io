# Surface brief: photography, endless canvas

Scope: `/photography/canvas/` (`_layouts/gallery-canvas.html`), one of the gallery versions the owner is comparing (kept unlisted with noindex until chosen). The owner's personal photographs ("just my life"). Visitor mode: Experience; the photographs lead. It shares the photo data, the close-up viewer and the studio with the other versions, and has its own visual world, separate from the scientific homepage.

Audience and job: friends, colleagues and visitors from the research site, browsing at leisure on a laptop or phone. They should feel they are looking around a wall of the owner's photographs through a camera, settling on one at a time, and be able to open any of them large.

Proof and content: photos published by the Gallery Studio into `_data/gallery.yml` (id, src, thumb, w, h, title, place, year, optional exif). Most photos have no title, place, year or camera settings; never show file names; never invent settings. Very little text.

Constraints: plain Jekyll as GitHub Pages builds it; photographs stay plain `<img>` elements in their true colours and their own aspect ratios; only the published watermarked copies are shown; no feed-like layout; no WebGL; reduced motion gets instant, still states; without JavaScript the same photographs appear in justified rows.

## Direction contract

THESIS: The gallery is an endless wall of photographs seen through a rangefinder camera's viewfinder. You look around it, and the camera frames, focuses and takes one photograph at a time. It refuses the scrolling grid and the full-screen slideshow.

OWN-WORLD: A near-black wall of photographs that repeats in every direction. A rangefinder viewfinder sits over it: warm bright-line frame corners and a faint full outline, a small yellowish focus patch showing a ghost of the picture, and a red seven-segment LED readout under the frame (DSEG7 digits over their unlit segments, Geist Mono labels) with a three-lamp focus indicator (two arrows and a dot) and a frame counter. Outside the frame lines the wall darkens at rest, as through an eyepiece. Name and footer in Schibsted Grotesk and Geist Mono.

STORY: The camera wakes (every segment and lamp lit for a moment), pulled back over the whole wall, then finds the newest photograph, closes in until it fills the frame and focuses; the frame lines, the darkening outside them and the focus patch come up only as that photograph glides into place (the owner: "when it first launch, the square edge is not correct"), and the patch never shows as an empty box. The wall's order is the gallery's: the newest year first, the latest added first within a year ("sort by year"). The visitor drags or uses the arrow keys to look around (Ctrl or Cmd + wheel moves to the next or the previous photograph, at the owner's ask); the faster the wall moves, the further the lens pulls back. When it slows, the nearest photograph is framed and focused. Zoomed out, clicking a photograph zooms back in on it (the owner: "when it's small when I click it will still enter the detail not zoom in"); at the framed size or closer, clicking a photograph opens it large at once, growing out of its place with no wipe across the screen (the owner: "I do t like a refresh from left to right"), and behind the close-up the wall frames the photograph clicked. Focus is shown and handed back only to keyboard users, so no label or ring appears after a tap or click. The large view is the frame, developed (the owner: "the gallery use the matching style and make it extremely cool"): the photograph alone on black inside the same bright-line frame (faint outline, bright corners), which closes in as the photograph pulls into focus; the same red LED readout under it (F, shutter, ISO, mm, frame number; every segment lit for a moment, then the digits roll and settle); a caption line (title, place, year) and the camera and lens in mono; and along the bottom the roll, every photograph as a frame on a strip of film with sprocket holes and its number printed on the edge. The strip is seen as in an open camera back (the owner: "it's no way to be the top of the film cage", then "upright side view"): it comes out of the felt light-trap slot of an upright metal cassette on the right (no brand; the knurled knob on top turns, with a red mark going round), passes under a fixed red film gate in the middle, and winds onto an upright take-up spool between two flanges on the left (the roll on it thickens as frames pass, its sprocket holes travelling round as it turns). It settles with one frame in the gate, which becomes the photograph shown: any deliberate swipe moves one frame (the owner: "the red box dynamically move to the central … when we left right sweep the film roll, it will auto move to last next photo … show a film on the left or right so when we drag the film it will rotate something just like a real film"). Any frame is clickable; a mouse can drag the film or turn it a frame per wheel notch. Swiping the photograph down closes the close-up on a touch screen ("When I sweep down I can exit this detail page"). Glass keys either side for previous and next, and Close; arrow keys and swipes step. Opening and closing move as an app does on a phone (the owner: "simulate how iOS open an app"): the close-up grows out of the photograph's place on the wall as a rounded window on a spring, and closing shrinks it back. Paging is a phone photo album's (the owner: "simulate Apple albums left right sweep's effect"): sideways the photographs lie side by side a gap apart, a drag moves them with the finger and the next comes in from the side, let go they glide on (a fifth of the way, or a flick) or back; arrows, keys and the roll slide the same way, and nothing fades. Every tap on the keys is answered at once (the owner: "The left right bottom is not responsive easily"): a step never waits on the network (the photograph arriving shows its small print until the full one comes, then sharpens), a quick run of taps keeps gliding from where it is, the counter and the roll move with the tap, and the keys give under the finger. This is the main photography page (/photography/), with "Enter the darkroom" (a small safelight-red dot before it) beside the name leading to the 3D darkroom, which links "Back to the wall". Zoom by hand (added at the owner's ask: "add a feature to zoom in and zoom out (touch use 2 fingers)"): a two-finger pinch zooms and pans at once; with a trackpad, pinch; with a mouse, the wheel itself ("can we use the mouse wheel directly to control zoom"), the - and + buttons in the corner above the footer, or the - and + keys (0 resets). Zoomed out, more of the wall shows and the frame lines close round the photograph, and the LED readout docks at the foot of the window on a solid plate, clear of the wall (the owner: "in the zoom out version we put it at bottom"); zoomed in, the photograph grows past them. It still comes to rest on the nearest photograph.

FIRST VIEWPORT: Full-bleed dark wall, pulled back to show many photographs; the bright-line frame centred; the LED readout under it showing all segments; then the newest photograph glides into the frame and the lens closes in until it fills it. The name top left (linking home), the photo count top right, a one-line footer with Email me, Homepage and Studio, and a one-line hint under the readout ("Drag to look around · scroll to zoom · click to open") that fades after the first move.

FORM: Endless canvas (the owner picked it from the brainstorm: "Endless canvas is good"), given the camera mechanism for "visually shocking, better from mechanism, retro and extremely cool". Signature interactions: speed-dependent pull-back; glide-and-close-in framing; a portrait photograph turns the camera, rotating the frame lines a quarter turn; the ghost image slides into register and the focus dot lights; the frame counter rolls like a mechanical counter. Each build stamps the page's scripts and styles, so a phone never runs the last deploy's. Camera settings show on the LED only when the photograph carries them. The bright-line frame keeps its square corners and the darkening is cut square round it; the focus patch in the middle is the rounded rectangle, as a rangefinder's is (the owner: "real viewfinder is actually rounded rectangle", then "I meant the inner rectangle is rounded, the outside is still original", then "The inner focus rectangle"). The photographs stay square. The camera's maker shows as its mark in the ink colour (Leica's dot in its red) before the model in the close-up and in the caption under the readout (the owner: "show camera logo somewhere like leica iphone hasselblad sony canon fujifilm nikon"): marks from Simple Icons (CC0) for Leica, Apple, Sony, Fujifilm, Nikon, Panasonic, Kodak, DJI, Google and Samsung; Canon, Hasselblad and others, which Simple Icons does not carry, are set as letter-spaced wordmarks rather than copied. A mark that is only a symbol (Leica's dot, Apple's apple, Google's G, Kodak's K) is followed by the maker's name, and a mark that spells the name (SONY, FUJIFILM, Nikon, DJI) stands alone (the owner: "After showing the camera icon still show the brand"). The name is set in lettering close to the maker's own ("use camera brands' font"): Apple in Apple's own SF where the device has it; Leica in bold capitals of Hanken Grotesk (OFL), a sober sans standing in for its licensed Corporate S; Hasselblad in light, widely spaced Hanken Grotesk capitals, as its wordmark. The makers' real typefaces are licensed and are not copied. Rendering: while the wall moves it is scaled as one layer; at rest the scale goes into each photograph's own size, so Safari (which keeps a scaled layer at the resolution it was drawn at) shows it sharp ("For iPhone the quality is so bad"). The darkening outside the frame is a plain veil with the frame cut out (clip-path), and the focus patch's ghost is plain translucent, with no blend mode (Safari washed photographs out while dragging). HDR photographs show standard range on the wall and glow only once framed and sharp (dynamic-range-limit). Reduced motion shows every state instantly with no inertia, speed zoom, turn or shutter (zooming by hand still works, at once).

FINISH: unreviewed and undocumented is unfinished; this build ends with the finish review, the verdict, DESIGN.md, and every shipping raster carrying its provenance
